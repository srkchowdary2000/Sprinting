# Recovery And Load Management Papers

## Haugen et al. 2019

Title: The Training and Development of Elite Sprint Performance: an Integration of Scientific and Best Practice Literature

Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6872694/

PMID: 31754845

DOI: 10.1186/s40798-019-0221-0

Type: Open-access review

Key points:

- The text says intensive sprint sessions need "at least 48 h" of recovery. Table 2 lists time to the next high-intensity session as 48h for acceleration, 48-72h for max velocity and sprint-specific endurance, and 24h for tempo.
- Sprint sessions should be ended when performance or technique drops.
- Sprint training load depends on distance, intensity, surface, footwear, rest periods, and total volume.
- The elite weekly template (Table 3) puts strength or plyometrics the day after each sprint day, then tempo.

Applied to your routine:

- Your 72h rule is safe but conservative. Acceleration days only need 48h.
- The issue is not just spacing; it is that all-out 100m reps are a very costly way to train every sprint quality.

## Van Hooren et al. 2024

Title: Optimizing Resistance Training for Sprint and Endurance Athletes: Balancing Positive and Negative Adaptations

Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608172/

PMID: 39373864

DOI: 10.1007/s40279-024-02110-4

Type: Open-access review

Key points:

- Resistance training has useful adaptations, but the dose matters.
- Excessive volume or hypertrophy emphasis can create tradeoffs for speed.

Applied to your routine:

- Put the gym session far enough from your highest-quality sprint day when possible.
- If gym is on the weekend, avoid maximal soreness from high-rep lunges, Nordics, or RDLs.

## Thomas et al. 2018

Title: Neuromuscular Fatigue and Recovery after Heavy Resistance, Jump, and Sprint Training

Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30067591/

PMID: 30067591

DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001733

Type: Crossover study (10 male athletes)

Key points:

- Compared three sessions: 15 x 30m max sprints, 10 x 5 back squats at 80% 1RM, and 10 x 5 jump squats.
- Fatigue resolved by 72h after the sprint and heavy squat sessions, and by 48h after jumps.
- Central nervous system (voluntary activation) deficits lasted only 24h after sprints (48h after squats). The authors conclude the fatigue is "not primarily underpinned by decrements in CNS function".

Applied to your routine:

- Your 72h figure for max sprinting is supported, but the cause is mostly muscle fatigue, not CNS fatigue.
- Heavy squatting needs about the same 72h. The gym day therefore counts as a hard day when you space your sprint sessions.
- These sessions were high-volume; your lower-volume sessions probably recover somewhat faster.

## Practical Recovery Rules

- Hard sprint days: 48-72h apart.
- Max velocity and sprint-endurance days: protect them the most.
- Lower-body gym: ideally 24-48h after a hard sprint day, not the day before max velocity.
- Leave about 72h between a heavy gym day and the next sprint day when you can (Thomas et al. 2018).
- Tempo: okay between hard days if it is truly easy.
- Static stretching: fine after training, but do not use long static holds before sprinting.
- Warm-up should be dynamic and progressively faster.

## Simple Readiness Check

Before a hard sprint session, do this:

- 3 easy build-ups.
- 2 accelerations at 80-90%.
- If legs feel flat, hamstrings tight, or stride rhythm poor, downgrade to tempo or technique drills.

Stop rule:

- Stop high-intensity reps if time drops by about 3% or mechanics visibly fall apart.
